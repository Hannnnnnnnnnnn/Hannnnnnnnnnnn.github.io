01

ATACZ PLP Revamp

A collection page redesigned to carry the brand — and an A/B test judged against criteria that were fixed before it ran.

Role
Product Design & A/B Analysis
Period
Jun – Aug 2026
Client
ATACZ
Tools
Figma, Shopify, GA4, Microsoft Clarity

Problem

Half of our paid visitors never see the homepage. Just over half of paid-social sessions land straight on a collection page, while only a fifth start at the homepage. For those visitors a product grid was doing the homepage's job — and saying nothing about who the brand is.

The proposal ruled one thing out at the start: the collection page is not the conversion bottleneck. Behaviour on it is nearly identical across channels, yet conversion differs about ninefold between paid social and search — so the gap sits downstream. No conversion lift was promised, and the report never claimed one.

Approach

A passive brand layer. A recycled-bottle counter on the first screen and a bottle badge on each product card, designed to be seen at the decision point without ever pulling visitors off the sales path. Alongside it: simplified product cards and a lighter filter control.

The direction shifted mid-flight, and the report says so. The proposal's combo banners were meant to cross-sell straps and accessories, but on a single-collection page those products are already on screen — so the three banners shipped as traffic-drivers to steady sellers outside the collection instead. That moved the average-order-value target out of scope: a scope decision, not a measurement failure. Adding things and removing things in one release also means a flat result can hide two opposing forces, which the report flags rather than hides.

The criteria were fixed before the test ran. The verdict was judged only against the proposal's own success measures. Other metrics were watched during execution but excluded from the verdict — which is what stops a verdict being assembled afterwards from whichever number happens to look good. US mobile decided the call; Canada's sample was too small to be precise. No early calls: the full 43-day window ran.

Result

Product click-through rose 8.2%, landing inside the 5–15% the proposal had targeted — direction and magnitude both matching the prediction.

Actual scroll distance rose 9.4%, the most statistically solid result of the test. This one nearly went unnoticed: raw scroll depth was effectively identical between versions, but depth is a percentage of page length, and the redesigned page was about 9.5% longer. The same percentage of a longer page means people were travelling further. Corrected for length, the back of the page got meaningfully more reach without losing anything at the front.

Nothing worsened. Dead clicks and quick bounce-backs both ran lower on the redesign, and the brand layer behaved as designed — seen by effectively everyone who loaded the page, clicked by almost no one, creating no exit path.

The banners were the interesting call. At 1.6% they sat inside the normal 0.5–3% range for informational banners with no offer behind them — not a failure. But in the same screen space a product card earns roughly six times the clicks, and a banner set has to be designed and maintained per collection. The recommendation was to tie banners to promotions rather than run them evergreen: a cost-versus-return call, not a performance problem.

Roll out the redesign. Apply banners selectively.

Verdict, A/B test report

The honest limit is stated in the report itself. At this page's traffic, confirming a change of this size to statistical significance would take six months or more, so 43 days matches the target rather than proves it. Nothing pointed the other way — the redesign never trailed in either period, and the scroll and friction measures moved with it. The recommendation that followed was to stop A/B testing pages this small and use before/after comparison instead.
